# FMGE Preparation Framework

> **Status:** Framework established  
> **Last verified:** 23 September 2026

## Purpose

This page provides a structured approach to preparing for the FMGE.

It is intentionally separate from the official examination blueprint.

The blueprint describes the examination structure. This page describes how a candidate can use that structure to organize preparation.

---

# 1. Start With the Official Blueprint

The FMGE blueprint contains **300 marks** divided into:

- **100 marks:** Pre/Para-clinical subjects
- **200 marks:** Clinical subjects

See:

[`blueprint.md`](blueprint.md)

The official subject allocation should be the starting point for deciding how much preparation time each subject receives.

---

# 2. Three Layers of Preparation

A useful preparation system can be divided into three layers.

## Layer 1 — Foundation

Build enough conceptual understanding to recognize and solve standard clinical and basic-science questions.

Focus on:

- Core concepts
- Definitions
- Classifications
- Mechanisms
- Anatomy
- Physiology
- Pathophysiology
- Pharmacology
- Common clinical presentations

---

## Layer 2 — Question Practice

Convert knowledge into examination performance.

For each question:

1. Read the stem carefully.
2. Identify what is actually being asked.
3. Generate the likely answer before looking at options when possible.
4. Eliminate incorrect options.
5. Select the best answer.
6. Review the explanation.
7. Record important errors.

The goal is not simply to increase the number of questions completed.

The goal is to improve the ability to recognize the tested concept.

---

## Layer 3 — Revision

Revision should progressively become more selective.

### Early revision

Review:

- Important concepts
- Difficult topics
- Incorrect questions
- Frequently forgotten facts

### Later revision

Prioritize:

- Repeated concepts
- Personal weaknesses
- High-frequency evidence
- Tables
- Algorithms
- Diagnostic criteria
- Image recognition
- Drug associations

---

# 3. Question Error Classification

When a question is answered incorrectly, classify the error.

| Error type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Knowledge gap | The concept was not known |
| Recall failure | The concept was known but could not be retrieved |
| Misinterpretation | The stem was interpreted incorrectly |
| Distractor error | An attractive incorrect option was selected |
| Calculation error | Arithmetic or formula error |
| Application error | The underlying concept was known but applied incorrectly |
| Careless error | The correct answer was known but execution failed |

This classification is useful because different errors require different interventions.

---

# 4. Active Recall

Important information should be actively retrieved rather than repeatedly reread.

Examples:

- Anki
- Self-testing
- Closed-book recall
- Practice questions
- Short-answer prompts
- Image identification

A useful rule is:

> **If you cannot retrieve it without looking at the answer, you probably have not consolidated it yet.**

---

# 5. Spaced Revision

Revision intervals should be adapted to the candidate.

A concept that is repeatedly recalled correctly can be reviewed less frequently.

A concept repeatedly forgotten should return sooner.

The project does not prescribe a single universal interval.

---

# 6. Subject Allocation

Preparation time should take the official blueprint into account.

However, subject marks alone should not determine the entire study schedule.

Other factors include:

- Current knowledge
- Previous exposure
- Question performance
- Topic difficulty
- Time remaining
- Personal error patterns

A candidate who is already strong in a subject may require less time than a candidate who is weak in the same subject, even when the official mark allocation is identical.

---

# 7. Topic Prioritization

Once sufficient question evidence becomes available, topics can be classified using multiple variables.

Potential variables include:

### Examination weight

How much of the official blueprint belongs to the subject.

### Observed frequency

How often the topic has appeared in documented evidence.

### Recency

How recently the topic has been observed.

### Repetition

Whether the concept appears across multiple examination sessions.

### Candidate weakness

Whether the individual repeatedly gets the topic wrong.

### Clinical importance

Whether the concept is important for safe medical practice.

---

# 8. Don't Confuse Frequency With Importance

A frequently observed question topic is not automatically the most clinically important topic.

Conversely, an important clinical topic may not have a high observed examination frequency.

The study guide therefore keeps:

**Exam evidence**

and

**Clinical importance**

as separate concepts.

---

# 9. First Pass

The first pass should aim for broad coverage.

Suggested objectives:

- Understand the major subjects.
- Build a basic conceptual framework.
- Complete an initial question exposure.
- Identify weak areas.
- Begin an error log.

Avoid spending disproportionate time trying to perfect a single topic during the first pass.

---

# 10. Second Pass

The second pass should become more question-oriented.

Focus on:

- Incorrect questions
- Marked questions
- Weak topics
- Repeated concepts
- Important classifications
- Diagnostic criteria
- Treatment algorithms
- Image-based questions

---

# 11. Final Revision

The final phase should emphasize retrieval speed.

Useful material includes:

- Short notes
- Error logs
- Anki
- Tables
- Algorithms
- Image collections
- Frequently confused concepts
- Personal weak areas

Avoid introducing large amounts of entirely new material immediately before the examination unless there is a specific reason to do so.

---

# 12. Mock Tests

Mock tests should be used as diagnostic tools rather than simply score generators.

After each mock, analyse:

### Score

How many questions were correct?

### Accuracy

How many attempted questions were wrong?

### Unattempted questions

Which questions were left because of uncertainty or time?

### Error type

Why were questions missed?

### Subject distribution

Which subjects produced the most errors?

### Topic distribution

Which topics produced the most errors?

---

# 13. The Feedback Loop

The preparation system should operate as a continuous loop:

**Study → Questions → Errors → Analysis → Targeted Revision → Questions**

This is more useful than treating question banks, notes and revision as completely separate activities.

---

# 14. Personal Weakness vs General Frequency

A candidate's study priority should eventually combine:

**General evidence**

with

**personal performance data**.

For example:

A topic may have moderate historical frequency but represent a major weakness for an individual candidate.

That topic may therefore deserve additional revision time.

Conversely, a frequently tested topic that the candidate consistently answers correctly may require less revision time.
